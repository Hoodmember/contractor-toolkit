Markup vs. Margin for Contractors: The Chart, the Formula and a Free Calculator

5-minute read · Pricing & estimating

If you add 25% to your costs, you are not keeping 25% of the price. You're keeping 20%. That difference between markup and margin is one of the most common reasons busy contractors end the year with less money than they expected.

The difference in one sentence

Markup is how much you add on top of your cost. Margin is how much of the final price you keep.

Job cost: $10,000 · Markup: 25% → Price: $12,500
Gross profit: $2,500 · Margin: $2,500 ÷ $12,500 = 20%

Same job, same dollars. The 25% and the 20% just measure against different numbers: markup against cost, margin against price.

The formulas

  • Markup needed = margin ÷ (1 − margin). For 25% margin: 0.25 ÷ 0.75 = 33.3%
  • Margin you get = markup ÷ (1 + markup). For 25% markup: 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 20%
  • Price for a target margin = cost ÷ (1 − margin). For $10,000 at 25%: $10,000 ÷ 0.75 = $13,333

What this costs you over a year

Say you do $200,000 of work and want to keep 25% as gross profit. Marking up 25% instead of 33.3% gives you about $40,000 gross profit instead of $50,000. That's roughly $10,000 a year gone, with the same crew and the same hours.

What margin should a contractor aim for?

It depends on your trade, market and overhead, so there's no single right number. Your gross margin has to cover all of your overhead (truck, insurance, phone, software, advertising, your office time) and leave a profit. That's why the right starting point is your own overhead, not a rule of thumb. Our hourly rate guide walks through it.

Three habits that fix this for good

  1. Decide on a target margin first, then convert it to markup with the chart above.
  2. Estimate from your real costs, including labor at its loaded rate (wage + payroll taxes + workers' comp).
  3. Check actual vs. estimated cost after every job so you know whether the margin you priced is the margin you got. See job costing for small contractors.
