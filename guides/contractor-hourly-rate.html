How to Calculate Your Hourly Rate as a Contractor or Handyman

7-minute read · Pricing & estimating

"What does everyone else charge?" is the wrong question. Two handymen in the same town can have completely different costs: one owns a paid-off truck and works from home, the other has a shop lease and an employee. The right rate starts with your numbers.

Step 1: Add up your monthly overhead

Overhead is everything you pay to stay in business that isn't billed to a specific job:

  • Truck payment, fuel and maintenance not billed to jobs
  • General liability and vehicle insurance
  • Phone, internet, software
  • Shop, storage or office rent
  • Tool replacement and repair
  • Advertising, website, lead services
  • Accounting, licenses, bonds

Multiply the monthly total by 12.

Step 2: Decide what you need to pay yourself

Your salary is a cost, not "whatever's left over." Pick a yearly number you'd accept working for someone else.

Step 3: Count your real billable hours

You don't bill 40 hours a week. Estimating, driving, supply runs, invoicing and callbacks eat time. Many small contractors bill closer to 25–32 hours per worker per week. Multiply by the weeks you actually work (allow for holidays, weather and slow periods).

Step 4: Price your labor at its loaded cost

An employee who earns $25/hr costs you more than $25/hr. Add employer payroll taxes, unemployment insurance and workers' comp. That "labor burden" is often 15–30% depending on your state and trade. Ask your insurance agent for your workers' comp rate.

Step 5: Do the math

Break-even rate = (annual overhead + owner salary) ÷ billable hours + loaded labor cost per hour
Target rate = break-even rate ÷ (1 − target profit %)

Worked example: 2-person crew

ItemAmount
Monthly overhead$3,050 → $36,600/yr
Owner salary goal$75,000/yr
Billable hours2 workers × 32 hrs × 48 weeks = 3,072
Overhead + owner pay per hour$111,600 ÷ 3,072 = $36.33
Helper wage + 20% burden$30.00
Break-even rate$66.33/hr
Target rate (15% profit)$78.03/hr

Anything under about $66/hr in this example loses money on every hour worked, even though it might feel like a "good rate."

Turning the hourly rate into job prices

Most jobs are quoted as a fixed price, not hourly. Use your rate to price the labor portion, add materials and subs with markup, and check the margin before you send the quote. Remember that markup is not margin.
